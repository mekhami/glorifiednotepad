I'm hooked on patches. I like the lowered bar - I don't need a fully ready to publish dungeon level to share with others, just 4-5 rooms, the _idea_ of what's inside them, as many details as I can feasibly shove in there tonight, and it's enough. If I throw together a map, all the better.

There's a tool that I think is a very good fit for this, called [Ex Umbra](https://sharkbombs.itch.io/ex-umbra) by Martin Nerurkar. It's a 1-4 player cooperative dungeon building game. I tried it out solo this evening, and while there is a bit of oddity playing it solo, it actually gave me everything I need for a dungeon patch, including a good rough draft of the map! In this post, I'm going to walk through my experience of using this game to make a dungeon patch, and then go over what I might change.

# Setup

Ex Umbra is played using a deck of custom cards, which I printed, cut, and sleeved in front of a standard playing card to make them feel like actual cards. However, you can just use a rollable table for this, if you're not inclined to doing some papercraft. I enjoy it. It also uses a few dozen of two different types of token. It doesn't matter what they are, so long as there are two different kinds, but the smaller the better. At first, I pulled some little bits out of a random board game from my board game closet, but in practice they were too big. These tokens are placed on the map to represent 'threat' and 'reward', so you want them to be small. Then I had a stroke of luck and found, in my daughter's craft supplies, a ton of tiny beads with letters on them, like the kind you would use to make friendship bracelets - so I had a few dozen tiny Ts and tiny Rs. Serendipity.

Then you need the paper you're gonna draw your map on, and some index cards.

That might seem like a lot of set up, but a lot of it is optional. You can even ditch the tokens if you're willing to just write your T and R tokens onto the map, but that means more erasing and less of a tactile bookkeeping experience, which was pleasant.

To begin, you set the size, aesthetic, and difficulty of the dungeon. This plays into how many turns the game lasts, which isn't really the most important part of the whole thing in my experience, but it gets you started somewhere.

Then you choose some aspects! Aspects sorta drive the whole theme of the dungeon. They establish your threats or challenges. My aspects were:

1. Corrupted Elements
2. Flooding Waters
3. Carnivorous Plants

I wrote these down on the index cards and put them on the table next to my map. Then, you draw the entrance to your dungeon on the map, and the setup is finished.

At this point, if you had more than one player, you take turns adding rooms, corridors, and features. As a solo player, you just do it on your own, which doesn't feel entirely natural, as though the game _really_ wasn't fully designed for solo play, but you get the hang of it. Just sorta take turns with yourself and follow the steps as it goes.

So you draw a room, then draw cards from the Ex Umbra deck. These cards have things like 'Blades', 'Chasm', 'Channels/Pipes', 'Mushrooms/Fungus'. Basically random elements. You take one of those cards and play it on one of the aspects. So, for instance, I got 'channels/pipes' and naturally put it on the **Flooding Waters** aspect, and now the first room has water flowing into it from rusting pipes.

You do this for as many cards as you have in hand and want to use, probably 2-3 features. Then you take Threat and Reward tokens for each feature you played. These tokens are used to turn these features into dangers, and to add treasures to some of them as well. So I might play a threat token and a reward token on these rusted pipes - now, they may start pouring toxic sludge, or flooding the area with water, and stuck within one of the pipes is a sachet of valuable jewels. The more threat and reward tokens you play on a feature, the more threatening and or/rewarding it is. With another reward token, that sachet of jewels becomes a giant pearl, and with another threat token, rot imps come pouring out to harass the party.

You continue adding features, rooms, corridors this way, playing cards from the deck, drawing more cards, until you get halfway through the number of turns that were determined by the setup, and then you activate the dungeon's trap card.

# Tremor

The tremor is where Ex Umbra takes the meat of what you did so far and throws a totally different spin on it. This was a fun moment in my game. I rolled on the Tremor table and got 'Wandering Monster' - It became a high threat, high reward beasty that colored the rest of my dungeon. I initially thought shark (obviously), but ended up going with a giant hermit crab inside of a huge brass bell. But the other elements on the tremor table can really invert what you've gotten so far with your dungeon, and encourage you to go back and revise previous features to adapt to this change.

After the Tremor is rolled and implemented, you go to the dungeon's 'Heart'. This is sorta like the boss room, but it doesn't necessarily have to have a boss encounter. It will be a room with high threat and high reward, and you're instructed to play random cards from the top of the deck, one on each of your aspects to find out what it is. You mix and match, making sure to use at least one of those card+aspect combinations, and come up with a set piece and an environment for a good, rewarding challenge.

After that, it's back to normal turns, until you run out of turns or tokens for your dungeon.

# Cleanup, and my Verdict

In the cleanup phase, you distribute whatever tokens you may be holding onto, close all the open pathways, cross the Ts and dot the Is, and of course, give your dungeon a name.

All in all, this was super fun. Here's what I came up with.

1. **Overgrown Altar** - Corroded pipes trickle water into what was once a place of worship. Inside one of the pipes is a giant pearl stuck in a glob of seaweed and muck. Ankle deep water on the lower level. On a raised level, where the altar would have stood, aggressive fungus pulsates, and looks to devour living flesh. The fungus eternally attempts to eat its way into the sarcophagus it sits on, within which is a sanctified corpse of a sea priest clutching a magical censer.
2. **Venus Fly Chasm** - This room is divided in half by a chasm, 60 feet wide. At the bottom of the chasm are carnivorous plants like venus fly traps, snapping at each other and anything that comes near. Something flickers golden underneath all the foliage. At the bottom of the chasm, a secret passage to [3].
3. **Statuary** - The long staircase to this room from [1] is lined with non-human faces carved into the walls. Pressure plate trap, when triggered, water flows out of the faces and anyone caught in it is swept back to [1]. In the statuary there are four alcoves and a statue in the center. The statues are of Mermen and Sahuagin fish-folk warriors. There's some sort of puzzle here, but I'm not sure what yet. The chasm from [2] extends into the northwest corner of this room, and is also filled with carnivorous plants.
4. **Piranha Pool** - This whole room is filled with water, 30 feet deep, and tons of piranhas. At the bottom of the pool is a trapdoor to another level of the dungeon, but it can't be opened from this side due to the weight of all that water. And the piranhas who are trying to eat you. 
5. **Shrine of the Sea Judge** - Another room filled with water, 60 feet deep, and this time a big ass shark. (I knew the shark was coming back!) The water is foul and oil slick, and the shark looks diseased and enraged. The statue in the center is decorated with several amphorae which would be pouring into the water, but the whole statue is overgrown with a creeping vine, dark green leaves and black flowers choking off the amphorae so that only a trickle of black liquid flows into the water. Removing the plant lets clean water flow again. At the bottom of the water is a chest of treasure including a magical grimoire.

I have a map, too! But I'd rather work on it some more than simply publish it. And some of these ideas still need work, like the statuary.

All in all, it's really not a bad starting place, and Ex Umbra took me less than an hour to play once I had everything assembled. I highly recommend it for getting the juices flowing. And don't forget, you have total authorial... authority... (I just realized those words are related, wow). So in the cleanup phase, you can just scratch out what's not working and add a better idea. It's good material for getting you past the blank page, though! I recommend it, and I'll be doing some more.
